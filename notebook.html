Why error logging works

Studies in cognitive psychology show that reviewing mistakes within 24 hours improves retention by 60%. The Error Vault forces you to confront your weak points, turning failures into stepping stones.

📘 Expand →

The method: Every time you make a mistake (in PYQ, module, or mock), write:

  1. The exact question and your wrong answer
  2. The correct solution and the concept used
  3. Why you made the mistake (conceptual gap, silly error, time pressure)

Why it works: This process activates deeper processing in the brain (elaborative rehearsal). By the third time you write the same type of mistake, your brain forms new neural pathways. Top rankers maintain an error notebook with 200–300 entries. Start with 5 per day.

Revision bonus explained

Marking all three subjects as “revised” gives +40 XP. This encourages holistic revision – many students ignore one subject, leading to lopsided scores.

📘 Expand →

Implementation: After your night reinforcement session (8–10 PM), open each subject's error log and actively recall the mistakes. Once you feel confident, click the subject tile. The system rewards you for the mental effort, not just for spending time.

Advanced tip: Don't mark a subject as revised unless you can verbally explain the mistake and correct solution. Use the Feynman technique – teach it to an imaginary student.

The science behind the mantra button

This button is a “tiny habit” trigger (BJ Fogg, 2019). The act of clicking releases a small dopamine reward (+5 XP) and provides an immediate command to start studying Physics.

📘 Expand →

How to use it: Place your phone or laptop near your study desk. Every morning at 7:28 AM, click the button, then immediately open your Physics module. The XP is trivial, but the behavioural conditioning is powerful. After 2 weeks, the button becomes a Pavlovian cue for “focus mode”.

Pro tip: Combine the mantra with a physical action – take a deep breath, then click. This anchors the state.

Why night reinforcement (8–10 PM) is crucial

The spacing effect (Ebbinghaus) shows that information is better retained when reviewed after a delay. The 8–10 PM slot is perfect because it is 10–12 hours after morning study, and just before sleep – which consolidates memory.

📘 Expand →

Optimal schedule: In the first hour, revise Physics mistakes from the morning session. In the second hour, go through Chemistry and Biology errors. Use active recall – cover the solution and try to solve again. This is called “retrieval practice” and is twice as effective as re‑reading notes.

Tool: Keep your error notebook (this page) open. Write new mistakes during the day, and review them in the evening.

Breaking the cycle of repeated errors

If you see the same mistake appearing 3+ times, it indicates a conceptual gap. Don't just write it – go back to the source (textbook/lecture) and re‑learn that concept.

📘 Expand →

Action plan: Create a “concept weakness” list separate from the error log. For each repeated mistake, write the concept name and schedule a 20‑minute revision session on Sunday. After that session, solve 5 fresh problems on that concept. This eliminates the root cause.

Example: If you repeatedly make mistakes in “rotational dynamics”, flag it. On Sunday, spend 20 minutes on theory, then solve 5 PYQs. You'll see immediate improvement.

Pre‑mock revision with error log

One day before a full mock test, go through your entire error notebook. Focus only on the mistakes you have made in the last 2 weeks. This is called “targeted revision”.

📘 Expand →

Method: Open the error log and cover the solution. Try to solve the problem again mentally. If you get it right, move on. If you still struggle, re‑write the correct solution and flag it for extra practice after the mock. Students who do this before every mock improve their scores by 20% on average.

Pro tip: Create a separate “mock errors” section in your error log. After each mock, transfer those mistakes to the main log. This creates a feedback loop.
